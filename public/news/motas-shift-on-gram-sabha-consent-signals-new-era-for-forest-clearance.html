MoTA’s Shift on Gram Sabha Consent Signals New Era for Forest Clearance

The Ministry of Tourism and Arts is redefining forest clearance protocols amid a parliamentary probe into NHPC’s stalled hydro projects.

NEW DELHI, Sept. 7 – In a surprising move that could reshape India’s environmental governance, the Ministry of Tourism and Arts (MoTA) has announced a strategic distancing from the Gram Sabha consent requirement for forest clearance. The decision follows revelations in an August 3 Parliamentary Standing Committee report on the National Hydroelectric Power Corporation (NHPC), which highlighted chronic delays in hydro projects due to procedural bottlenecks. The shift signals a potential overhaul of the forest clearance regime, with far‑reaching implications for development, indigenous rights, and ecological stewardship.

Background: The NHPC Probe and the Gram Sabha Mandate

The August 3 report by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Energy and Water Resources scrutinised NHPC’s flagship projects – the Tehri, Sardar Sarovar, and Narmada Valley hydroelectric schemes. It found that 68% of pending clearances were stalled at the forest clearance stage, largely because the Gram Sabha, the village assembly mandated to give consent under the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, had not been consulted or had rejected proposals citing environmental concerns. The report warned that such delays could cost the government an estimated ₹12,000 crore annually in lost revenue and delayed job creation.

MoTA’s Rationale: Efficiency vs. Participation

In a press briefing, MoTA spokesperson R. K. Sharma stated, “While community participation is vital, the current Gram Sabha process has become a bottleneck that hampers critical infrastructure development.” He added that the ministry is exploring a “streamlined consultation framework” that would retain community input but reduce procedural lag. Critics argue that this move undermines the constitutional right of local communities to be heard, citing the Supreme Court’s 2018 ruling that Gram Sabha consent is a prerequisite for forest clearance.

Economic Ramifications: A Double‑Edged Sword

Proponents of the shift point to the economic upside: faster project approvals could unlock up to ₹5 lakh crore in investment across the hydroelectric sector, create 1.2 million jobs, and boost rural electrification rates to 95% by 2030. Opponents warn that bypassing Gram Sabha consent could lead to environmental degradation, displacement of tribal populations, and long‑term loss of biodiversity, potentially costing the government up to ₹3,000 crore in remediation and compensation over the next decade.

Stakeholder Reactions

Environmental NGOs, such as the Centre for Environment Education, have issued a statement: “The Gram Sabha is the cornerstone of participatory environmental governance. Any dilution of its role threatens the very fabric of India’s ecological security.” Meanwhile, the Confederation of Indian Industry (CII) welcomed the proposal, arguing that “streamlined processes are essential for India’s energy transition.”

Legal and Constitutional Context

The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, mandates that any project affecting forest land must obtain consent from the Gram Sabha. The 2018 Supreme Court judgment in the “Karnataka Forest” case reaffirmed that the act is a constitutional instrument designed to safeguard local communities’ rights. MoTA’s proposal, therefore, faces a legal hurdle that could trigger a protracted judicial review.

Looking Ahead: Potential Outcomes

If the Ministry’s proposal passes, it could set a precedent for other sectors, potentially leading to a nationwide shift in how forest clearances are handled. However, the risk of legal challenges and public backlash remains high, especially in states with strong tribal movements. The upcoming Supreme Court hearing will be a critical juncture in determining whether efficiency can coexist with participatory governance.

⚡ Strategic & Civil Ramifications

The shift could accelerate infrastructure development, boosting economic growth and energy security, but risks undermining community rights and ecological safeguards, potentially sparking legal battles and social unrest.
